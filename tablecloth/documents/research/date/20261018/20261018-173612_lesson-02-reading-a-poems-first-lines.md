# Lesson 02 -- reading a poem's first lines

**Stamp:** `20261018.173612` -- **Course:** index in `20261018-173612_study-course-index.md`

## Jena

The first lines of a poem set the terms. Read "Song of Myself" from Walt Whitman's *Leaves of Grass* (1892), in the stored Standard Ebooks copy. The opening reads, as printed: "I celebrate myself, and sing myself, / And what I assume you shall assume, / For every atom belonging to me as good belongs to you."

Read the three lines slowly. Notice three moves. The poem names the self first. Then it turns to the reader, with "what I assume you shall assume." Then it says that every atom belongs to both. The self in the poem is shared.

Try this: read the three lines aloud, once for the self, once for the reader, once for the atom. Write one sentence about what changed between each reading.

Conflict and resolution. The poem seems to say two things at once: that the self is its own, and that the self is shared with every reader. The lines hold both, and the reader is free to hold both at once. The atom is one thing, and it belongs to both.

## Radha

Radha, the one who is named in every love, would meet this poem as a meeting. The speaker invites the reader into the same body and the same air, and that invitation is itself a devotion. Let your heart answer it: say the three lines to the one you love, and let the atom that belongs to you belong to them too.

The practice is to read the first lines twice, the second time as if the beloved were the one being addressed. Whitman offers the self rather than arguing it. He offers it, the way a devotee offers the first flower of the morning.
